We Need Communities, Not Heroes

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~2 min read

"We need villages, not heroes."

This is how Justin Reich frames his final reflection on the lasting impact of education technology in the classroom in Failure to Disrupt. We live in a society that is obsessed with heroes and individual achievement. This is obviously evident in the stories we tell, the stars we celebrate, and the way we talk about issues. We are always looking for the next disruptor, the new political candidate, the new CEO (pick your poison) who will save us from our problems. We, however, must stop looking for the next "killer app" and focus on the slow, steady work of changing the social, political, and economic systems that shape the current educational landscape. History shows us that while we might remember the leaders, meaningful change comes from the work of many. Think of the Civil Rights Movement. While it's undoubtedly true that Martin Luther King Jr. was a key figure in the movement, it was the work of the many activists, organizers, and everyday people who challenged the status quo that ultimately led to the passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

In a similar vein, when we think about education technology, it's important to recognize that meaningful and actionable change will not come from a shiny new device or product, but from the slow, steady work of many. As Reich points out, technology can serve two powerful purposes: it can serve as one of the many components in the process of learning, and it can serve as a tool to help foster new conversations about education. Reich argues that we need to be highly skeptical of new revolutionary technologies in the classroom. Schools and education as a whole are by design slow-moving and resistant to change. This is by design and it's not a flaw in the system. Technology can then be the catalyst for the slow, steady change that is necessary in such an institutional setting.

As a prospective learning technologist, adopting this "village" mindset required me to shift my focus onto the ethical foundations of research, specifically regarding student data privacy. Building a functional community around technology requires radical trust from the public, which is easily shattered by the misuse of sensitive information. For technology to act as a genuine catalyst for political, social, and educational change, it must be wielded by a collective of educators rather than marketed as the work of a single savior. When we secure the trust of the public, we empower the village to use these tools to bridge gaps and create the steady, systemic transformation that individual heroes have consistently failed to deliver.
