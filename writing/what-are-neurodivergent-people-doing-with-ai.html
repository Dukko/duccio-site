What Are Neurodivergent People Doing with AI?

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~4 min read

And why has nobody planned for any of this?

Open /r/autism (a support Reddit community for autistic people) and I bet you will see a variation of this: “Help me ask my [boss/professor/friend] for [thing] without sounding rude.” This request may seem small or even silly to a non-autistic (or neurotypical) audience. But these are real issues neurodivergent people experience every day. And once again, disabled people found their own solution.

When asked what they need, autistic people often self-report “help me advocate for myself” as their top focus. Easy enough, right? Not so much. Noticing something is wrong, deciding it is worth asking about, finding the words, and following up when the answer is insufficient or vague? For neurodivergent students, this might as well be a herculean job.

Most research on AI and neurodivergent learners has focused on writing and planning, but I kept finding a common third use that researchers were not really looking for. Students are using large language models as tools to practice their advocacy skills in a safe and controlled environment.

This makes a ton of sense, in retrospect. The advantage students reported in a recent study was not that the quality of the help received from a chatbot was particularly good, but that they were always there. Human advocates and disability resources have limited office hours that require planning and scheduling. Super easy stuff for neurodivergent students! Chatbots? They’re open 24/7, even at 3am, while you’re ruminating on that text you sent 5 hours ago.

This is particularly obvious when you see how neurodivergent folks discuss AI. A recent research study identified twenty distinct uses of AI being discussed, two of which are exactly what we’re talking about: re-drafting a message in a tone and language a neurotypical user can understand clearly, and rehearsing conversations before having them.

The thing is that nobody designed these tools for these uses. Neurodivergent folks found the use, wrote the prompts, and shared them with each other to support their advocacy journeys.

You might wonder: “Why should anyone outside of the disability bubble care about this?” I think that if you value rights and equity, this is pretty important. Self-advocacy is the shaky bridge between legal rights and actual support. Accommodations might be codified in law and written on paper, but someone has to request them, explain them, and re-explain them to each instructor or manager. Especially if these leaders don’t have the knowledge to understand what words like scaffolding, wait time, and other neurodivergent-specific jargon means. The problem is usually a mismatch between how a neurodivergent person communicates and how a neurotypical-focused institution receives the message.

Advocacy is a costly and complicated process: each step requires attention, patience, working memory, and most importantly, the energy to be polite when told no. Chatbots are not designed to teach anyone to advocate, sure, but they do lower the initial cost for a neurodivergent person. And this can be life-changing.

This is also where the trouble starts. These models, not designed for this specific task, default to a specific register: professional, hedged, slightly apologetic, some could even call it “corporatese.” It can take real effort for a neurodivergent person to get the output of the chatbot to not sound like a canned marketing email. Why does that matter? Well, it’s asking students to perform a version of themselves that doesn’t fit and sounds inauthentic, which can directly harm their self-advocacy efforts.

And what will happen once the AI bubble pops, and these tools become less accessible? If a student is always outsourcing the task, do they ever learn the skill? There’s research showing that overuse of LLMs can actually atrophy the very brain paths needed to build the skills.

The real question we need to answer as a society is what a good use of LLMs looks like. There’s a lot of conversation about AI psychosis, cognitive surrender, and a lot of similar fearmongering. But there’s also a space here for custom-designed tools that enable neurodivergent self-advocacy. As a society, will we step up to the task and embrace this new generation of accessibility tools with a critical and cautious look? Or will we continue to leave this task to informal peer support groups that might lack the necessary skills or time to do this in a safe and smart way? The choice is up to us, I believe.
