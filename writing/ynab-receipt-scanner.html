Teaching an app to read a receipt

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~5 min read

I use YNAB for my budget1, and I hate typing out a Costco run line by line almost as much as I love the app itself. A single receipt might be groceries, a household item, and something for the dog, and to keep my budget sane, I want that split across three categories, not dumped into one. So I built a small self-hosted tool that photographs the receipt, has an AI vision model read and categorize every line, and creates the split transaction for me. This is the post-mortem on that build, and on why the hardest part turned out to be the same problem I solve at work.

The problem: a generic receipt reader isn't enough

The easy version of this tool reads a receipt and guesses at categories: "Costco" becomes "Groceries," done. That's fine until it isn't. My actual budget doesn't use YNAB's default categories, it uses the ones I've spent years customizing, split and renamed to match how I actually think about money. A generic reader that maps to generic categories is exactly as useless as a generic form applied to two different licensure tracks: it produces an answer, just not the one that's useful for my specific setup.

The principle

A tool that outputs a plausible answer instead of the specifically correct one just moves the cleanup work later, it doesn't remove it.

The build

1. Capture cheaply, on the device that's already in my hand

The whole thing is a self-hosted PWA: point a phone camera at a receipt or pick one from the gallery, and the image gets compressed client-side before it ever leaves the phone. No app store, no native build, just something I can add to my home screen and use standing in a parking lot. Often, you don't need to reinvent the wheel of overcomplicate things.

2. Give the AI my actual categories, not a generic list

Before the image is sent anywhere, the app pre-fetches my real YNAB category list in the background. That list goes to the model alongside the photo, so it isn't guessing at a plausible category name, it's matching line items against categories that actually exist in my budget. Claude or Gemini reads the receipt, groups items, and returns splits using my exact category names. That's the same move as the Hardcover recommender: don't ask the model to reason in the abstract, give it the real, specific structure it needs to be right for one particular person's setup.

3. Nothing becomes a transaction until I say so

The AI's output lands on a confirmation screen, not directly in my budget. I review the parsed splits, fix anything wrong, and only then does it create a standard YNAB transaction, or subtransactions if the receipt spans categories. The model does the tedious first pass. It does not get to touch my actual financial record unsupervised.

The principle

AI output becomes a record only after a human who knows the data confirms it. Convenience is the AI's job, trust is still mine.

Where this connects to NXT GEN

I built this on my way home from Costco (using voice to text, I am a safe driver!) to stop hating grocery runs, but it landed on the exact principle behind the data infrastructure work I did rebuilding our program's dashboards. There, two years of spreadsheets had drifted apart because summaries were being generated by hand from data that no longer matched. Here, a receipt reader that guessed at generic categories would have quietly drifted from my real budget the same way. Both problems have the same fix: give the AI the real, current, specific source of truth to work from, whether that's my actual YNAB categories or a program's actual licensure standards, instead of letting it reason from something generic and close enough.

The confirmation screen matters for the same reason our dashboard rebuild's verification rule matters. Our director's standing rule is that no number leaves the team until a person who knows the candidates has signed off on it. This app follows the identical shape at a much smaller scale: no line item becomes a real transaction until I've looked at it. In both cases the AI earns trust by being checked, not by sounding confident.

The principle

Whether the record is a grant report or a grocery split, AI is only as trustworthy as the specific data it's grounded in and the verification step before it counts as real.

What I'd take from this back to work

  • Feed the model your real, current structure, not a generic default. Mine is a category list; at work it's a licensure standard.2 The specificity is what makes the output usable.
  • Keep AI output one step away from the system of record. A confirmation screen at home is the same idea as a review pass on an AI-populated dashboard.
  • Small, boring automations are worth building. This one just saves me a few minutes a week, but it's the same instinct that made the dashboard rebuild worth the effort: remove the busywork, not the judgment.

If you're building small tools to automate the parts of your own life you actually hate, I'd enjoy comparing notes. Find me on LinkedIn, or see the code on GitHub.

1. I've since moved on to ZeroSum, so this app and the budget it was built for are both retired. I'm leaving the post as written rather than rewriting it into the present tense. Not everything you build needs to outlive the thing it was built for, and it's fine to let a tool go once it's done its job.

2. Obviously, that "real, current structure" never includes anything confidential. No candidate names, no student records, nothing that would touch FERPA, goes anywhere near a model. What gets fed in is the shape of the data, category names, standard codes, field structure, never the protected information sitting inside it.
