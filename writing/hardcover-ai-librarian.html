Building a librarian who actually knows my taste

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~6 min read

I read a lot, I log all of it in Hardcover, and I still can't get a recommendation that isn't a whiff. "Readers who liked this book also liked" is based on popularity, which is the issue with generic recommendation systems. It's built for the median person who read that book, and I have found I am reliably not the median person. So over a week in early February I built my own recommender: Hardcover AI Librarian, a small self-hosted app that reads my actual library and uses an LLM to suggest books, verified against real catalog data before it ever shows me a title.

This is the post-mortem on that build, and on why it kept reminding me of my day job.

The problem: recommendations built for the average reader

Every recommendation engine I triedeither gave me generic recommendations based on a single data point (a book) or treated my library as a bag of titles to be matched against everyone else's bags. That works fine if you want the safest, most averaged-out next read. That's not what I was looking for. Incidentally, I realized It's the same failure mode as a single generic form trying to serve two very different teacher licensure tracks: one instrument, one signal, applied to people who don't actually have that much in common. The aggregate flattens exactly the thing that makes a recommendation useful, which is that it's for me, specifically, based on what I've actually rated and tagged and reread.

The principle

An average built from all the data points tells you almost nothing about the one person in front of you.

The build

1. Turn a reading history into an actual taste profile

The app pulls my full "read" shelf from Hardcover's GraphQL API, including my ratings, the community rating, tags, and release year, and feeds the sixty most recent reads to the model as a taste profile, alongside a hard exclusion list of everything I've already finished. The point wasn't to summarize my library. I wanted to give the LLM enough real, individual signal that it could stop guessing and start reasoning about a specific reader: me.

2. Let AI reason over that data, not guess

I wired up three providers, Gemini, GPT-4o, and Claude, so I could pick the model doing the reasoning, and gave it my filters: mood, genre, page count, year range, minimum rating. The AI's job is narrow and specific: given this person's actual taste data and these actual constraints, propose ten books and explain why each one fits. That's the useful role for AI in something like this. It finds patterns and makes connections across a taste profile faster than I ever could by hand, but the profile itself is real data, not just guesswork.

3. Verify everything before it reaches me

LLMs invent books. Titles that don't exist, authors attached to the wrong novel, plausible-sounding nonsense. So every recommendation gets checked against Hardcover's own search index in parallel, and anything with fewer than three community ratings gets dropped. Only verified, real, database-sourced books make it to the results table.

The principle

An AI-generated solution is only as trustworthy as the actual verifiable data you check it against. Confidence isn't truth.

Where this connects to NXT GEN

I didn't set out to build a metaphor for my job, but by the second evening I noticed I was solving the same problem twice. At NXT GEN Teach, the entire premise of a registered apprenticeship is that a paraprofessional's path to licensure has to be individualized: their starting skills, their licensure track, their classroom context, and their pace are all different, and a generic pathway serves no one well. That's exactly why, for example, we split one progress report into two instruments instead of forcing ABS and ECSE candidates through the same form. The instinct is identical to the one behind this app: stop optimizing for the average person and build the pathway around the actual data you have on the individual.

The verification step matters for the same reason in both places. We don't let a coaching note or a progress rating stand on its own; it has to trace back to an actual licensure standard, the same way this app won't show me a book unless it traces back to an actual catalog entry. In both cases, the AI or automation gives you a real efficiency advantage, but it has to be tethered to ground truth or it just produces confident-sounding noise, at a larger and faster scale than a human would.

The principle

Individualized pathways and AI-assisted insight both fail the same way when they lose their connection to real, specific data about the person they're for.

What I'd take from this back to work

  • Personalization is a data problem before it's a modeling problem. The app is only as good as the taste profile it's given, the same way a coaching plan is only as good as the holistic data behind it.
  • AI is best used narrowly: reasoning over real, specific data toward a real, specific person, not guessworking its way to a generic answer and hoping it fits.
  • Any system that generates an individualized recommendation, whether it's a book or a next coaching move, needs a verification layer against actual verifiable sources, not just a plausible-sounding output.
  • The instinct to reject "one size fits all" is worth trusting even when it means more work, because it was right both times I followed it this year.

If you're thinking about where AI actually helps in a program built around individualized pathways, versus where it's just automating the average, I'd enjoy comparing notes. Find me on LinkedIn, or see the code on GitHub.
