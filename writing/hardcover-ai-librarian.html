Building a librarian who actually knows my taste

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~6 min read

I read a lot. I log it all in Hardcover. The hope? I would get personalized recommendations! And I still get recs built for someone else. What gives? (Update: Hardcover now shipped Vibes, which is their personalized recommendation engine. So far, it is pretty good. So this is a historical piece now!)

This is the post-mortem on that build, and on why it kept reminding me of my day job.

The problem: recommendations built for the average reader

Open any "readers also liked" list and you will see the problem. It aims at the median person who liked that book, which makes sense, except I am reliably not that person.

Easy fix, right? "Just build your own recommendation engine then, Duccio", in a sarcastic tone. So in early February I did. A small self-hosted app that reads my real library and asks an LLM to reason over it. Then it checks every suggestion against real catalog data before it shows me anything.

Most engines fail the same way. They match my shelf of titles against everyone else's pile. That gives you the safest next read. But that is often not the right one for me.

This was more than a reading problem. It was a data problem.

The principle

An average built from all the data points tells you almost nothing about the one person in front of you.

The build

1. Turn history into a taste profile

It pulls my full read shelf, ratings, tags, year, plus a hard exclude list of finished books. It feeds the last sixty to the model. The point is not to summarize. It is to give the model enough real signal to reason about one reader.

2. Let AI reason, not guess

I wired Gemini, GPT-4o, and Claude behind filters for mood, genre, length, and year. The job is narrow. Given this taste and these limits, propose ten books and say why each fits. Narrow prompting helps LLMs reason better.

3. Verify before you show

LLMs tend to invent sources. So every title gets checked against Hardcover search in parallel. Fewer than three community ratings and it drops. No match, no show.

That last step is what makes it trustworthy. Confidence is not truth. And trust must be based on truth.

The principle

An AI-generated solution is only as trustworthy as the actual verifiable data you check it against. Confidence isn't truth.

Where this connects to NXT GEN

You might wonder why this matters outside books. At NXT GEN Teach we hit the same wall. We split one progress report into two because ABS and ECSE are different jobs. One form flattened both. Same instinct as the app. Stop optimizing for the average. Build around the actual person.

What I'd take from this back to work

So where does AI help here? When it reasons over real data for a real person, and when something verifies it. Everywhere else it just automates the average faster.
