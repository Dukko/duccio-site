Failure to Disrupt: Beware of the Hype Men

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~3 min read

If you paid attention to the field of education and technology in the early 2000s, you would have been hard pressed to miss the incredible and often mystical-sounding promises made by tech evangelists. Traditional in-person schools are going to disappear! Democratize education through online learning! Many of these slogans were seen as self-evident truths in the field of edtech. Fast forward to a few years later, and here's a chilling piece of data to consider: "Early research reports showed that MOOC [Massive Open Online Courses] completion rates typically hovered around 5 percent." Quite a low number, compared to the incredible promises made by the hype men of online learning. What happened?

Justin Reich, in his book Failure to Disrupt, posits a simple explanation: the Matthew effect is once again at play. This effect, also known as the cumulative advantage principle, is the idea that an initial level of success leads to increased future gains, while an initial disadvantage leads to further losses. Or, in other words, the rich get richer and the poor get poorer. The author doesn't mince words when it comes to his perspective on online learning:

"[…] evidence suggests that an edtech Matthew effect is quite common: that new technologies disproportionately benefit learners with the financial, social, and technical capital to take advantage of new innovations."

What Reich is saying is that online learning has been a boon for the Western students who have access to the technology and the resources to take advantage of it, but it has been a complete letdown for the rest of the world. It is particularly fascinating to note that this remains true even when accounting for differences in education levels, income, and socioeconomic status: "MOOC researchers have identified a 'global achievement gap' in which learners from the most affluent countries earn certificates at substantially higher rates than learners from the least developed countries, even adjusting for individual education level and other factors."

What is the lesson to be learned here? In my opinion, it is not that online learning is a bad idea, but that the idea of edtech as the solution to educational inequity is at best misguided and at worst dishonest. Educational challenges are complex and multifaceted, and while technology can be a powerful tool for addressing some of the specific challenges faced by students, it is not a panacea or a silver bullet. As a tech enthusiast, I am always on the lookout for new and innovative ways to use technology in education, but I am also painfully aware of the limitations and challenges that come with implementing such tools in institutional settings, which are by definition resistant to change. Suffice to say, I am extremely excited to see how Mr. Reich continues to explore this topic in the rest of the book.
