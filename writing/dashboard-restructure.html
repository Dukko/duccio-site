Rebuilding program data infrastructure with AI

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~4 min read

The problem: spreadsheets that disagreed

A registered teacher apprenticeship program generates data from every direction: recruitment contacts across dozens of districts, applicants moving through admissions, enrolled candidates with placements, mentors, and funding sources that all need tracking. By our second recruitment cycle, that data lived in overlapping spreadsheets maintained by different people, and they disagreed. At one point our admitted-candidate count changed depending on which tab you looked at. In a team meeting our director said it plainly: she was not sure we could trust any of it. When a program is reporting to a federal grant and a state licensing board, that is not a small problem.

The fix: a three-dashboard architecture

I quickly realized that the fix was not another, better spreadsheet. I needed to design a new architecture. Over a series of working sessions, my team and I designed a three-dashboard system with clear ownership and a defined handoff between each stage of the pipeline. A district partnership dashboard tracks every partner contact with a status view: confirmed, in progress, stalled, brand new. A recruitment dashboard holds every prospect from first contact, consolidated into one sheet with year tabs so history is never overwritten. An all-programs dashboard is the operational source of truth for enrolled candidates, including per-term funding columns so our licensure and finance colleagues see the same numbers we do. The rule that made it work is boring on purpose: a candidate moves from one dashboard to the next only at a defined moment, and the person adding them tags the person tracking them. A simple handing of the baton that now seems obvious, but was simply not there before. Saved filter views replaced destructive filtering, so anyone can pull a count by stage without wrecking someone else's work.

Where AI did the heavy lifting

AI is what made the migration feasible. Reconciling two years of conflicting records by hand would have taken days of side-by-side squinting. Instead I set up the clean structure and had my AI assistant do the heavy lifting: pulling every candidate out of the legacy sheets into the new tabs, cross-checking the tallies that never matched, and building year-over-year comparison slides directly from the raw records rather than the summary tabs that had drifted. The comparison surfaced real news, including that our apprenticeship and residency enrollment had roughly doubled between cycles, and it explained why our old summaries had been wrong. In one meeting I caught myself saying it out loud: it is like actually having an assistant. Heh.

What keeps it trustworthy

Two design choices matter for anyone trying this in their own program. First, AI output goes through human verification before it becomes the record. Every AI-populated sheet got a review pass from the person who knew the candidates, and our director's standing rule is that no number leaves the team until it is verified. Second, the summaries now auto-populate from the source data, so the reporting layer cannot move away from the truth layer again. The point of automation here was never speed for its own sake. It was making a small team's data trustworthy enough that a director can send growth numbers to college leadership, or a grant report to a funder, without a week of discussions first.

The principle

AI earns trust by being verified, not by being confident. Reconciliation only counts once a human who knows the data has signed off on it.
