Rebuilding program data infrastructure with AI

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~4 min read

The problem: too many cooks

If your registered teacher apprenticeship is anything like mine, it generates data from every direction: recruitment contacts across dozens of districts, applicants moving through admissions, enrolled candidates with placements, mentors, and funding sources, just to mention a few things. By our second recruitment cycle, that data lived in overlapping spreadsheets maintained by different people, which can be fine, I guess. Except, ours had drifted out of sync. At one point our admitted-candidate count changed depending on which tab you looked at. In a team meeting our director said it plainly: she was not sure we could trust any of it. When your director tells you she has a problem with your data, you have a problem.

The fix: we need to start this over

I quickly realized that the fix was not another, better spreadsheet. Or, well, kinda? I needed a better spreadsheet system. Over a series of working sessions, my team and I designed a three-dashboard system with clear ownership and a defined handoff between each stage of the pipeline. A district partnership dashboard tracks every partner contact with a status view: confirmed, in progress, stalled, brand new. A recruitment dashboard holds every prospect from first contact, consolidated into one sheet with year tabs so history is never overwritten. An all-programs dashboard is the operational source of truth for enrolled candidates, including per-term funding columns so our licensure and finance colleagues see the same numbers we do. The rule that made it work is boring on purpose: a candidate moves from one dashboard to the next only at a defined moment, and the person adding them tags the person tracking them. A simple handing of the baton that now seems obvious, but was simply not there before. Saved filter views replaced destructive filtering, so anyone can pull a count by stage without wrecking someone else's work. And, more importantly, everyone is looking in the same place. No more "Duccio's version" or "the old sheet."

Where AI played its role

AI is what made the migration feasible. Reconciling two years of conflicting records by hand would have taken days of side-by-side squinting and a lot of coffee. Instead, since I stopped drinking coffee, I needed some help. Turns out, LLMs can be excellent assitants. I set up the clean structure and had my AI friend do the heavy lifting: pulling every candidate out of the legacy sheets into the new tabs, cross-checking the tallies that never matched, and building year-over-year comparison slides directly from the raw records rather than the summary tabs that had drifted. The comparison surfaced real news, including that our apprenticeship and residency enrollment had roughly doubled between cycles, and it explained a few kinks in the previous system which explained why our old summaries had been wrong. In one meeting I caught myself saying it out loud: it is like actually having an assistant. Heh.

What's the lesson regarding AI

It would have likely be easier to just tell chat to whip up a new spreadsheet. Two design choices were paramount for me, though, and I think they will matter for anyone trying this in their own program. First, AI output ALWAYS goes through human verification before it becomes the record. Every AI-populated sheet got a review pass from the person who knew the candidates, and if we were not sure, we verified it before assuming. Second, the data summaries we use to report now auto-populate from the source data, so our external-facing numbers are always accurate. The point of automation here was never speed for its own sake. Automation's value is when it creates systems that simplify and amplify the human work of the team. No amount of LLMs can replace the deep knowledge of our candidates that the recruiter has established over several get-to-know-you calls, or the navigator's understanding of the program's requirements and the candidate's progress.

The principle

AI is valuable when it is verified and when it amplifies knowledge, not when it attempts to replace it.
