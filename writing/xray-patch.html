Good idea, wrong home: a post-mortem of my KOReader patch

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~9 min read

I've been dabbling in KOReader customization for a while, and one of the most interesting plugins I found was xray.koplugin. It already lets an AI identify characters/places/terms in whatever book you're reading in KOReader. It's essentially the X-Ray feature Amazon built on their Kindles, but AI powered and free. For the kind of reading I do, it was already doing a lot of things right. What I found myself missing was the final step in the puzzle: a way to know who/what an entity was without leaving the page. A couple of evenings later, I built the Entity Footnotes patch to underline those entities right in the text with a tap-to-reveal AI description, reusing the same tap interaction the plugin already had for unit conversions. That part is a little techy, but the more interesting part is that the maintainer of the plugin said yes to the idea and no to the location, which is the real story here.

This is the post-mortem on that patch, on the "no" that reshaped it, and on why solving it right meant not touching the plugin at all.

The problem: focused reading, not distracted tapping

As I mentioned above, ultimatejimmy made a very strong plugin. The plugin already did a lot of things right: it let you scan the book for entities, and it let you tap on them to get more information. But it was missing one key thing: a way to surface that information in-line while reading, without having to leave the page. A straightforward version of the feature would have been to scan the entire book at once and populate the entity list, but that would have felt wrong in practice because X-Ray doesn't work that way. Entities are discovered progressively as the AI scans further into the book, not all at once. Can you imagine if you got a pop-up at the beginning of the book that told you a character is going to die in chapter 10? That would be a pretty terrible experience. So the solution had to be more nuanced than simply scanning once and being done with it.

The principle

The real shape of a problem only shows up once you look at how the data actually arrives, not how you assumed it would.

The build

1. Don't fork it, don't ask to merge it — patch it at runtime

Forking the plugin was my first instinct, and in this case it was the wrong one. Forking the whole code would have meant that I would have to maintain it myself, and that would have been a lot of work for essentially a small feature. The maintainer's "no" was useful in a very practical way: it forced me to think about the problem from a different angle, and it led me to the solution of patching the plugin at runtime instead of forking it. KOReader is a Lua-based system, and Lua is a very dynamic language. That made it possible to change the behavior of existing code at runtime, which is exactly what I did with the Entity Footnotes patch.

2. Put the update lifecycle where the meat actually is

Because of this architecture decision, the patch is essentially a separate entity from the plugin itself. It can be updated independently, and it doesn't require any changes to the plugin's codebase. This means that my work as a maintainer of the patch is completely separate from the maintainer of the plugin. In other words, I have freed up the maintainer of the plugin from having to worry about the update lifecycle of the patch, and I have taken on that responsibility myself. In turn, making it a small responsibility in itself, ensuring I can support it while doing other stuff (i.e. my day job, parenting my kid, etc.).

3. Keeping hardware constraint in mind, not just the feature

I quickly realized that there's an additional reason the original plugin doesn't scan the entire book at once: e-ink devices are slow, and scanning the entire book at once would have been a very slow operation. My initial version did just that, and it was a terrible experience. The solution was to scan the book in chunks, and to do it in the background, so that the user could continue reading without being blocked by the scanning process. I could have ignored this constraint and just built the feature I wanted, but that would have been a bad experience for the user, and ultimately, the user experience is what matters most.

The principle

The right system boundary isn't only about the features; it's about scalability and the end-user experience. You can always add features, but you can't always fix a bad experience.

Things kept breaking

  • Bug 1: Whole book scanning hangs the whole experience. You can't just build in abstract.
  • Bug 2: Infinite rescan loop on a book with zero matching entities. If you don't test thoroughly, a small fix can have unintended consequences.
  • Bug 3: Localization keys leaking as raw text in the UI. Polish is important. That's what drives the user experience.

I won't bore you with the details of each bug, but the point is that they were all real, specific constraints that I had to work around. Most importantly, I had to decide what to do about the rescan loop bug: try to fix it, or accept it as a known limitation. I chose the second option, accepting the tradeoff of case-sensitive matching to reduce false positives, even if that meant ALL-CAPS text would be missed. That is a compromise for future Duccio, but for now the patch is stable and usable. And that is what mattered most to me.

The principle

Sometimes you can't fix all the problems, but you can choose your failure mode on purpose.

Taking "no" as data, not a rejection

The original PR was a simple enough idea on paper: make the feature feel more native by letting it hook into the plugin's own scan lifecycle. The maintainer's objection was not a vague "not now". It was a very specific concern about repeated rescanning becoming a UX commitment they did not want to guarantee across every e-ink device out there, with all their different performance profiles and quirks. That was exactly the kind of feedback that deserves respect, because it was about the real experience of using the software, not about ego or scope. So I didn't try to win the argument by pushing harder. I took the boundary seriously and built the patch around it instead: keep the experience useful, keep the plugin's ownership intact, and let me absorb the operational complexity of this patch without making the maintainer sign up for it.

I also tried to meet the project where it already was. X-Ray itself was built by ultimatejimmy, and the patch I wrote leaned into the same instincts the plugin already used: reusing its existing monkey-patch approach instead of inventing some parallel workflow or new abstraction. That felt like a form of respect, too. Not because it made the patch more official or more likely to get merged, but because it let me work inside the system the maintainer had already designed, on their terms, and leave the project's shape intact.

The principle

Sometimes, a "no" is a precise description of a boundary, and the collaborative move is finding the shape that fits it, not arguing for your solution.

Where this connects to my day job

The same lesson showed up in my day job at NXT GEN Teach. When I think about teacher candidates, I don't think the job is to design interventions around some idealized version of who they are supposed to be, or what a "good" candidate can be expected to do in a perfect week. It is to look at the actual conditions they live under: their schedules, their energy, their obligations, their access to support. Only then do you shape the intervention around that reality. If a support system only works in the abstract, it will fail the people it is meant to serve, and that is exactly the kind of mistake I tried to avoid in the patch.

The principle

Good design starts by respecting the situation a person is actually living, not the idealized version we wish they had.

What I'd take from this back to work

  • Start by treating the problem as a real-world constraint problem, not a clean abstract one.
  • When you design a system, make it fit the conditions people actually work under, not the conditions you wish they had.
  • Build with the people who will use it, and make room for their feedback so the fix can survive real life.
  • The strongest interventions are usually simple, practical, and shaped by the messiness of actual practice.

If you’ve made it this far, you’re probably either actually interested in the patch or simply the sort of person who enjoys taking a small annoyance and making it behave better. If that sounds like your kind of fun, the patch is ready to try, the code is here on GitHub, and the original discussion is over on xray.koplugin#100.
