One performance, one page: designing my mentor training as a real instructional design artifact

Duccio Mondanelli · 2026 · ~9 min read

I have spent two years building a professional development curriculum for mentor teachers: nine asynchronous modules, a facilitator guide for four synchronous sessions, seven printable coaching handouts, scenario banks, knowledge checks. It teaches experienced special educators how to coach a teacher candidate instead of evaluating one. It is, by any honest definition, instructional design work.

And almost none of it reads as instructional design work to someone outside my program. It lives in Canvas pages. It assumes a cohort, a facilitator, and a program calendar. There is no design document, because I was the designer, the SME, and the facilitator, and I never needed to hand the thinking to anyone else. Which is exactly the problem: a portfolio artifact is a handoff. It has to carry the reasoning on its own.

The test this piece has to pass isn't "can he run a mentor program?" — that much was never in doubt. It's "does he think like an instructional designer?" A recruiter in learning & development has four minutes and no context for my program, my state, or special education. The artifact has to make the design visible on its own terms: a clear performance, a single page of aligned decisions, an assessment that verifies a decision rather than a definition. That is the lane, and this is the thing that unlocks it.

Disclosure first: I didn't build this. I don't have an Articulate Rise license, and the real training still lives in Canvas and ProServa — that's where mentors actually take it. What I made is the part that survives a handoff: a design spec for a standalone micro-course, plus a one-page design doc that runs audience → objectives → alignment → assessment. Anyone with Rise could execute the spec, but the thinking is the deliverable. This post is about the decisions that took, in order, because the doc is the fast part and the decisions are the real work.

What I started with

The source material is Coach's Corner, the mentor PD series I built for a special education teacher apprenticeship program. Year one orients new mentors: the shift from evaluator to thinking partner, a feedback model called Celebrate-Guide-Grow, three observation lenses mapped to High Leverage Practices, a Skill vs. Will diagnostic. Year two goes deeper into the hard version of the job: observing a resident for a full year without evaluative authority, which means learning to collect low-inference evidence — what a video camera would record — and let the resident interpret it before you do.

It works in context. But "in context" is the operative phrase. The modules lean on discussion boards, a shared vocabulary built across months, and a facilitator who reads the posts before every live session. You cannot drop a hiring manager into module three and expect the design to be visible. The design was distributed across a year. The artifact needed it concentrated.

Scoping: pick a performance, not a module

My first instinct was to convert a full module. That instinct was wrong, and catching it early saved the project. A module is a container shaped by my program's calendar. A micro-course should be shaped by a performance: one thing the learner does differently afterward, small enough to teach in twenty minutes and concrete enough to assess.

The performance I chose is the post-observation debrief. It is the moment the whole curriculum orbits: a mentor has just watched a candidate teach, and what happens in the next ten minutes decides whether the candidate thinks or defends. Running that conversation well requires three separable skills that my Canvas modules teach across two years — leading with evidence instead of judgment, letting the candidate interpret first, and choosing a coaching move that matches what the candidate needs. Compressed and re-sequenced, that is a complete, self-contained micro-course, which I specced as Coaching the Debrief. It also generalizes. Nothing in it depends on my program, my state, or special education specifically. Any mentorship-based preparation program has this exact conversation.

The principle

Scope the artifact to a performance, not to your existing content structure. The container you built for a cohort is almost never the right container for a standalone course.

The one-page design doc

Here is the document, lightly compressed for the web. It is one page on purpose. The discipline of one page is the point: every row you cannot fill is a decision you have not actually made yet.

Course

Coaching the Debrief: giving feedback that makes new teachers think. A ~20-minute micro-course specified for Rise with one Storyline branching interaction. This is a design spec, not a build — no Rise license. The real training still ships through Canvas/ProServa.

Audience

Experienced K–12 teachers serving as mentors or cooperating teachers for a student teacher, resident, or apprentice. Experts in teaching; novices in coaching. Their default mode — trained by every rubric and evaluation they have ever received — is to watch a lesson and deliver a verdict. Time-poor: the course must be completable in one sitting, on any device, with no facilitator.

The gap

Mentors give feedback that is either vague praise ("great rapport!") or an unsupported conclusion ("your pacing was too fast"). Both end the candidate's thinking. Candidates comply or defend; they do not reflect. The debrief produces agreement or friction, not growth.

Terminal objective

Given an observed lesson, the mentor will conduct a debrief in which they (a) present low-inference evidence before any interpretation, (b) prompt the candidate to interpret that evidence first, and (c) respond with a coaching move — Celebrate, Guide, or Grow — matched to what the candidate's reflection reveals.

Alignment

Enabling objective Content Practice Assessment
1. Distinguish low-inference evidence from interpretation, and rewrite an interpretation as evidence. Side-by-side examples ("You don't call on students equitably" vs. "You called on 8 students in 12 minutes; six sat in the front two rows"), plus the disagreement test: if two observers could have written different things, it's interpretation. Rise sorting block: classify eight statements as evidence or interpretation. Given three evaluative statements, select the strongest low-inference rewrite of each; feedback explains what the wrong options smuggle in.
2. Sequence a debrief so the candidate interprets the evidence before the mentor does. The five-move debrief: candidate reflects → mentor shares one piece of evidence → candidate interprets → mentor offers their view, marked as one view → land on a concrete next step. Rise ordering interaction: rebuild a scrambled debrief transcript into the correct sequence. Spot-the-break: a debrief transcript with one sequencing error (mentor concludes before the candidate speaks); learner identifies the move that broke it.
3. Given the candidate's reflection, choose the matching coaching move and compose a response consistent with it. The three buckets — Celebrate (build confidence with specific evidence), Guide (question toward the why), Grow (commit to a concrete next step) — and how to read which one the moment calls for. Three short scenarios; learner picks a bucket and sees a model response in that bucket. Storyline branching conversation: a full debrief with a candidate whose reactions change based on the mentor's choices. Wrong moves aren't "incorrect" — they show the candidate going quiet or defensive, and the scene lets you recover.

Assessment philosophy

No scores. Every assessment is scenario-based and feedback shows consequence, not correctness: choose the verdict-first opener and you watch the candidate stop talking. Completion equals reaching a reflective outcome in the branching debrief. A downloadable one-page job aid (the five-move sequence plus bucket cues) is the takeaway artifact, because the real assessment happens in their next debrief, where I can't see it.

What the alignment table exposed

I expected writing the doc to be a formatting exercise. It was an audit. The moment content, practice, and assessment sit in adjacent columns, you can see what your course actually verifies — and my original Canvas knowledge checks did not survive the look. They tested recall: "The Celebrate bucket is primarily designed to…" A mentor can answer that perfectly and still open their next debrief with "so, that transition was rough." Recall was never the gap. The gap was performance under the pull of old habits.

So every assessment became a decision inside a scenario. This is also where the Storyline block earned its place: a branching conversation is the closest a self-paced course gets to the actual performance environment, because the thing that makes debriefs hard is not knowing the model — it's holding onto it while a real person reacts to you.

The principle

If the assessment column just restates the content column with a question mark, you've built a reading with a quiz. Assess the decision, not the definition.

Tooling decisions: Rise for the spine, Storyline for one moment

I can't author in either tool — no license, no build. So read this the way a spec gets read: it's the tooling decision I'd hand whoever builds it, and for design purposes the decision itself is the point, because it's a judgment about where the expensive interaction earns its cost.

The split is pragmatic. Rise handles the spine: reading blocks, sorting, ordering, scenario cards. It is fast to build, responsive by default, and honest about what it is. Storyline appears exactly once, for the branching debrief, because that interaction genuinely needs state — the candidate's disposition has to persist across choices for the consequences to feel real.

I considered specifying the whole thing in Storyline for portfolio signaling. I decided against it, and I'd defend the decision in an interview, which is rather the point: reaching for the heavier tool everywhere is its own design smell. The spec should show judgment about when the expensive interaction is worth its cost, not just that I can describe one.

What the design trades away

Honesty section. "Conversion" is the wrong verb, since I never built it. The real frame is what this design gives up next to the thing that's actually running in Canvas and ProServa. That original leans on what a standalone micro-course cannot have: discussion boards where mentors read each other's real observation notes, a facilitator who names patterns across the cohort, and the fact that every participant has a live candidate to practice on that same week. A micro-course trades all of that for reach and self-sufficiency. The branching scenario and the job aid are replacements, not equivalents — social accountability became simulated consequence. Because I specced rather than built, this is also the cost review I'd otherwise only have done after a build. The design doc says so in one line, because a doc that hides its trade-offs is marketing.

What I'd tell someone building their first ID artifact

  • Start from work you actually did. A portfolio piece invented for the portfolio has no constraints in it, and constraints are where design is visible.
  • Scope to one performance, twenty minutes. The restraint is the demonstration.
  • Write the design doc before you open the authoring tool. Mine took an evening and changed the assessments completely; the build is just execution.
  • Make the alignment table load-bearing. Every objective gets content, practice, and assessment in the same row, or it gets cut.
  • Let feedback show consequences. "Incorrect, try again" teaches test-taking. A candidate who goes quiet teaches coaching.
  • Name what the format costs you. Reviewers trust a designer who states trade-offs more than one who claims none.
